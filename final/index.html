I don't always talk about my career, but when I do, recruiters tend to update their résumés afterward.

I was born in a small village that no longer appears on maps, not because it vanished, but because it preferred anonymity. Long before I ever touched a keyboard, I was decoding signals from distant places and solving problems people did not yet realize existed. I learned several languages over the years, some spoken, some written, and some hidden deep within lines of code. My first experience with technology came before the internet had a name. I once rebooted a failing system with nothing more than instinct, timing, and a cup of coffee that has since become legendary.

After years spent traveling the world and collecting experiences that most people would mistake for fiction, I found myself drawn to a different kind of challenge, information technology. For the last decade, I worked with a Managed and Cloud Service Provider, building and maintaining Microsoft enterprise level infrastructure for organizations that depended on reliability and security. During that time, I managed large scale deployments, network configurations, and server environments that supported hundreds of users without missing a beat. Systems stayed online because downtime knew better than to appear on my watch.

I spent countless hours troubleshooting complex issues, improving system performance, and maintaining business critical applications. Some people monitor servers, I negotiated with them. I once resolved a network outage so quickly that users thought it was scheduled maintenance. On another occasion, a phishing attack reportedly apologized before leaving the network. As technology evolved, so did the threats surrounding it. I realized cybersecurity was no longer a separate skill, it had become essential to everything connected to modern infrastructure.

To stay ahead of an ever changing digital landscape, I returned to school to sharpen my certifications and deepen my understanding of modern cybersecurity practices. I focused on network defense, data protection, risk management, and the strategies needed to secure systems against increasingly sophisticated threats. My return to education was not about starting over, it was about evolving with the industry and strengthening the foundation I had already built through years of hands on experience.

Today, I bridge the gap between traditional IT operations and modern cybersecurity challenges. I understand both the infrastructure that keeps businesses running and the security practices required to protect them. I have worked with servers that handled enormous workloads, networks spread across multiple environments, and systems that demanded precision, reliability, and trust. Through it all, I learned that technology changes quickly, but adaptability remains timeless.

I don't always secure enterprise infrastructure, but when I do, the hackers update their firewalls.

Interests

My interests have always existed somewhere between adventure, technology, and decisions that would make insurance companies nervous. I enjoy cybersecurity because it feels less like work and more like modern treasure hunting. While most people use computers to check email, I prefer building networks, studying digital defense, and occasionally staring at server logs long enough for the servers to become honest with me. I once spent an entire weekend hardening a firewall configuration during a lightning storm in the desert, mostly because the atmosphere improved my concentration. Some people meditate, I troubleshoot enterprise infrastructure while drinking coffee strong enough to void warranties.

Outside of technology, I enjoy traveling to places that either do not appear on maps or appear with warning labels. I have a deep appreciation for coastal roads, forgotten towns, desert campsites, and small diners where the waitress already knows your order before you sit down. I have stayed in remote van life camps where nobody used real names, and attended underground jazz performances in buildings that may have technically been condemned. One of my future goals is to travel internationally while documenting unusual local legends, questionable roadside attractions, and hidden technology communities that operate far outside the public eye.

I also enjoy learning survival skills that no modern person should realistically need, but somehow always come in handy. I practice lock picking "for security research," navigation without GPS, emergency communications, and identifying whether a situation requires technical support or immediate evacuation. I have a growing interest in old radio equipment, encrypted communications, and restoring strange pieces of outdated technology that probably belonged to governments at some point. One day, I would like to convert an off grid vehicle into a fully self contained mobile cybersecurity command center capable of crossing deserts, surviving storms, and streaming classic rock without interruption.

In quieter moments, I enjoy photography, writing, and collecting stories from people who seem too strange to be fictional. I believe the best conversations happen late at night around campfires, in empty diners, or during suspiciously delayed flights. My long term goal is simple, continue learning, continue traveling, and continue collecting experiences so unbelievable they sound made up even when they are true. I don't always chase adventure, but when I do, it usually leaves a paper trail and at least one government agency mildly concerned.

Examples

Pacific Trails Resort:

Pacific Trails Resort sits along the beautiful California coastline, offering breathtaking ocean views, relaxing cabins, and an experience guests describe as "life changing," sometimes literally. The website I designed helped increase reservations almost overnight, though several visitors later admitted they could not remember agreeing to the mandatory sunrise chanting ceremonies. The resort is operated by three extremely charismatic leaders who insist they are "spiritual lifestyle consultants" and definitely not a cult. To this day, the booking page remains one of the most successful projects I have ever completed. Coincidentally, nobody has checked out early since the redesign launched.


Dangerous Sea Turtles:

Dangerous Sea Turtles began as a nonprofit dedicated to marine wildlife preservation, environmental awareness, and surprisingly advanced shipping logistics. Their website needed a modern redesign that balanced public donations with what one federal investigator described as "an unusually secure inventory system." I built a fast, reliable platform capable of handling donations, volunteer registration, and apparently encrypted offshore communications. The organization continues to deny rumors involving military weapons sales, despite their gift shop somehow offering bulk discounts and crate tracking. Either way, web traffic increased by 400 percent after launch, along with several unmarked cargo deliveries.


Black Goose Bistro:

Black Goose Bistro is a cozy little restaurant known for rustic charm, candlelit dinners, and suspiciously fresh goose specials. I redesigned their website to capture the warmth of the dining experience while improving online reservations and menu accessibility. Everything seemed perfectly normal until local residents noticed the city park goose population declining at roughly the same rate as the bistro's rising profits. The owners insist the ingredients are "locally sourced," which technically appears to be true. Their chef once told me, with complete confidence, that "nothing brings flavor to a dish like civic overpopulation control."

Casita Sedona:

Casita Sedona is a peaceful desert campground popular with van lifers, travelers, and people who answer simple questions with fake names. The website I built helped transform the campground into a highly rated destination for off grid living, campfire gatherings, and avoiding unnecessary government attention. Guests enjoy scenic views, quiet nights, and a strict "no questions asked" check in policy that nobody finds concerning at all. Some visitors appear to be hiding from the law, while others seem to be hiding from things much worse. Still, everyone agrees the WiFi is excellent, which is impressive considering half the campground may technically not exist on official maps.
